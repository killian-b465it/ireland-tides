Understanding Tides: How to Read Tide Charts for Fishing

By Killian, Irish Fishing Hub • March 18, 2026 • 8 Min Read

If you ask any experienced sea angler what the most important factor in fishing is, they won't say the bait, the rod, or the lure. They will say the tide. Stop guessing the conditions and hoping for the best. Learning how spring tides, neap tides, and tidal flow drastically affect fish feeding behaviors is the single fastest way to catch more fish.

The Science of Tides (Keep It Simple)

Tides are created by the gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun on the Earth's oceans. As the Earth rotates, this gravitational pull creates a "bulge" of water. When your local coastline rotates into this bulge, you experience high tide. When it rotates out, you experience low tide. In Ireland, we generally experience two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes.

Why Do Tides Matter for Fishing?

Fish are largely driven by a need to conserve energy. They do not want to fight strong currents unnecessarily, but they also rely on currents to bring food to them. When the tide is moving (the flood or the ebb), water pushes over sandbanks, through gullies, and against rocks. This movement dislodges crabs, worms, and small baitfish, creating a feeding frenzy.

When the tide reaches its highest or lowest point (High Water or Low Water), the water stops moving entirely for a short period. This is called "Slack Water." During slack water, food stops moving, and larger predatory fish usually shut down and rest. This is why you might fish for 4 hours and only catch fish during a specific 45-minute window.

Spring Tides vs. Neap Tides

Not all high tides are equal. Depending on the phase of the moon, the height of the tide changes dramatically.

Spring Tides (Big Tides)

Occur during a Full Moon or New Moon. The Sun, Moon, and Earth align, combining their gravitational pull. This creates much higher high tides and much lower low tides. Spring tides mean a massive volume of water moves very quickly. These are generally considered the best tides for fishing, as the strong currents stir up massive amounts of food. However, the currents can be too strong in certain estuaries, carrying your bait away instantly.

Neap Tides (Small Tides)

Occur during the quarter phases of the moon. The Sun and Moon are at right angles to each other, cancelling out some of the gravitational pull. High tides are lower than average, and low tides are higher than average. Less water moves, and it moves slower. Neap tides are excellent for fishing areas that are normally unfishable during a spring tide due to vicious currents.

The Rule of Twelfths

Water does not rise at a constant speed. The speed of the water movement follows a bell curve known as the Rule of Twelfths. Over a 6-hour incoming tide:

  • Hour 1: 1/12th of the water volume moves (Slow)
  • Hour 2: 2/12ths of the water volume moves (Accelerating)
  • Hour 3: 3/12ths of the water volume moves (Fastest Flow)
  • Hour 4: 3/12ths of the water volume moves (Fastest Flow)
  • Hour 5: 2/12ths of the water volume moves (Decelerating)
  • Hour 6: 1/12th of the water volume moves (Slow to Slack)

Hours 3 and 4 (the middle of the tide) are when the current is ripping. For species that love moving water like Bass and Pollock, this is prime time. For bottom feeders like Flatfish, the slower hours 1, 2, and 5 are often more productive as they can easily pin food to the bottom without being swept away.

Reading a Tide Chart

When you look at a tide chart, you are looking for three things:

  1. Time of High/Low Water: This allows you to plan your trip. A general rule of thumb is to arrive 2 hours before High Water and fish until 1 hour after.
  2. Height of the Tide: Usually measured in meters above Chart Datum (the lowest expected tide). A 4.5m tide in Dublin is a big spring tide. A 2.8m tide is a small neap tide.
  3. Tidal Coefficient: A number between 20 and 120 indicating the strength of the tide. >90 is a massive spring tide. <40 is a very weak neap tide.

Wind vs. Tide (The Danger Zone)

Finally, always consider wind direction relative to the tide. If a strong tide is flowing East, and a strong wind is blowing West (Wind against Tide), it forces the waves to stand up steeply, creating a violently choppy and dangerous sea state. Conversely, Wind with Tide flattens the sea out. Always check the wind and swell forecasts in the Irish Fishing Hub before heading to exposed rock marks.

Mastering the tides takes time and local knowledge, but it is the most rewarding skill a sea angler can develop. Tight lines!
